Local Coordinates Are Sufficient: Visual Navigation & Pose Generalization

  • Reviewer mSNH: Q3

Why local coordinates are sufficient

We transfer coordinated motion through action alignment in local coordinates; the policy uses visual navigation to reach the appropriate position and orientation, and pose generalization to adapt its actions to variations in the robot’s position and orientation. Sec. 3.3 ↗

Visual navigation

In Pull Curtain, successful executions Task demonstrations ↗ illustrate learned turning and forward motion, including when to stop. Among 40 trials, 2 fail from turn-timing errors and 1 from early stopping (7.5%) Failure analysis ↗. These observations support the policy’s ability to guide whole-body movement through vision.

Pose generalization

In Open Fridge, successful executions Task demonstrations ↗ show that the policy can complete the task despite small variations in the robot’s position and orientation Evaluation setting ↗. Among 40 trials, 2 fail from heading drift and 1 from hand misalignment (7.5%) Failure analysis ↗. Performance under pose perturbations also improves with more training data Data-scaling results ↗. Conclusion

For the tested tasks, action alignment in local coordinates is sufficient for motion transfer, with visual navigation and pose generalization allowing the policy to adapt execution to the robot’s position and orientation.

Object Forces & Closed-loop Tracking

  • Reviewer mSNH: Q3
  • Reviewer vfta: W1, Q2, S1

Why it works without simulated object forces

We perform offline action alignment in simulation without object interaction forces. The aligned motions are then executed by SONIC, whose closed-loop feedback corrects tracking deviations caused by external forces.

External-force test

We apply 0–20 N opposing forces while replaying the same motions with the same SONIC controller. At 20 N, position errors change from 1.80 to 2.09 cm in Open Fridge and from 2.78 to 3.72 cm in Pull Curtain.

Conclusion

Tracking accuracy changes only modestly under the tested external forces, supporting SONIC’s ability to compensate through closed-loop feedback. This supports the effectiveness of our offline action alignment despite not simulating object interaction forces, within the tested force range.

Study Scope & Visual Generalization

  • Reviewer mSNH: W3, Q4

Evaluation setting

We train one policy per task. Testing uses the same objects and scenes, with random perturbations to object (e.g., laptop) and robot poses (positions and orientations). Appendix B.5 ↗

Research focus

We study whole-body loco-manipulation skill transfer from egocentric human data. Generalization to unseen objects, scenes, or tasks and instruction following are outside this research scope.

Data collectors

All demonstrations are collected alternately by two collectors.

Pose generalization

Performance under pose perturbations improves with more training data (Fig. 4 ↗), supporting within-task visual generalization. Our fixed-image ablation ↗ further supports the role of visual feedback in action prediction.

Zero-shot scope

As stated in the abstract and Secs. 3.1/4.1, zero-shot means transfer to the robot without target-task robot demonstrations, after task-specific training on human demonstrations. It does not refer to unseen-task generalization. Abstract ↗ · Sec. 3.1 ↗ · Sec. 4.1 ↗

Coordinated Whole-body Motion Transfer

  • Reviewer mSNH: W1
Sec. 3.3 ↗Appendix D ↗Table 14 ↗Figs. 9–11 ↗

Motivation: Transfer coordinated whole-body motion from human demonstrations.

Approach: Replace EgoHumanoid’s decoupled controller with SONIC’s whole-body tracker.

Problem: Direct online IK control conflicts with SONIC’s whole-body tracking and disrupts balance in the tested variants.

Solution: Use offline action alignment to preserve SONIC’s feedback control during execution.

Online IK failure examples

We test different degrees of mixing direct online IK control with SONIC control. All three variants remain unstable, supporting our choice of offline action alignment.

Visual alignment: EgoHumanoid uses depth-based reprojection and inpainting for visual alignment. We use a simpler, effective approach to reduce viewpoint gaps: similar camera heights and image augmentation. We additionally use robot-arm rendering to address appearance differences.

Hardware & Capture Setting

  • Reviewer mSNH: W1
  • Reviewer fQQ5: W1, Q1
Sec. 3.2 ↗Fig. 2 ↗

Advantages

Action: We record human actions with VR motion capture. Unlike HuMI/BifrostUMI, we do not require handheld grippers or matched robot grippers. This supports dexterous hands and avoids gripper-related hardware and setup costs, with potential for scalable collection.

Vision: We use only egocentric images instead of wrist views, providing body-centered scene context for loco-manipulation, such as Pull Curtain.

Challenges

Action: Differences in human/robot hand and body geometry make action alignment more demanding.

Vision: Different camera viewpoints and human/robot arm appearances create substantial visual gaps.

Contributions

Action: Two-stage offline action alignment: K followed by D.

Vision: Visual alignment combines similar camera heights and image augmentation for viewpoint differences with robot-arm rendering for appearance differences.

Conclusion

A direct comparison without accounting for these different hardware settings and action and visual alignment challenges would be unfair.

Action Alignment with a General Controller

  • Reviewer mSNH: W1
Sec. 3.3 ↗Table 1 ↗

Advantages

We reuse the same frozen, open-source SONIC controller across tasks, supporting scalable transfer without task-specific controller training.

Challenges

Controller endpoint tracking errors are a major source of the action gap. Training or tuning a controller on downstream demonstrations can reduce this gap and ease action alignment, as in HuMI. BifrostUMI also trains its own tracker. Reusing a general controller requires addressing these errors without downstream controller adaptation.

Contributions

Our two-stage offline action alignment compensates for the action gap without tuning SONIC, retaining a shared, reproducible controller.

Conclusion

Scalable transfer should not depend on retraining the controller for each new task. Comparisons must therefore account for whether downstream controller training reduces the action alignment burden.
